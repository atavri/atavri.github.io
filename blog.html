NISAR Is Now Observing: New Opportunities for Antarctic Sea Ice

With L-band observations now becoming part of the polar remote-sensing landscape, NISAR creates new opportunities to connect microwave scattering with sea-ice structure, melt processes, and conditions in the marginal ice zone.

The launch of the NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar mission, NISAR, introduced something particularly exciting for sea-ice remote sensing: regular, large-scale L-band SAR observations of Earth's ice-covered regions.

NISAR launched on July 30, 2025 and entered its science operations phase in early 2026. For polar researchers, the mission adds a longer radar wavelength to an observing system that already includes sensors such as Sentinel-1, ICESat-2, SWOT, and passive microwave radiometers.

Why L-band matters

Radar frequency controls how microwave energy interacts with the snow and ice system. C-band and L-band therefore do not necessarily respond to the same physical properties in the same way.

My doctoral research focused on exactly this problem: understanding how C- and L-band radar observations respond to sea ice during the advanced melt season.

The key lesson was not that one frequency is universally "better" than another. Instead, different wavelengths can provide complementary information about surface and volume scattering as snow, melt ponds, roughness, and ice structure evolve.

The opportunity

Combining radar frequencies can help separate physical changes in the sea ice from changes that simply appear different because of the way a particular sensor observes the surface.

Why Antarctica is especially interesting

Antarctic sea ice presents a particularly interesting application because snow conditions, flooding, deformation, melt, and fragmentation can all modify the microwave signal.

These processes are especially important in the marginal ice zone, where the ice cover interacts directly with ocean waves and changes rapidly across space and time.

Passive-microwave sea-ice concentration products provide the long climate records that we depend on to monitor the polar oceans. But the algorithms used to produce these records can disagree in heterogeneous ice conditions and during periods when surface properties change rapidly.

High-resolution observations from SAR provide an opportunity to investigate why these disagreements occur rather than treating retrieval uncertainty simply as a statistical error.

The value is in combining sensors

For me, one of the most exciting aspects of NISAR is not NISAR alone. It is what becomes possible when its L-band observations are combined with other measurements.

Sentinel-1 provides complementary C-band radar observations. ICESat-2 provides information related to surface elevation and ice geometry. SWOT introduces Ka-band observations at another very different microwave wavelength. Passive microwave radiometers provide the large-scale concentration records used extensively in climate studies.

Each measurement sees a different part of the physical system.

Together, they create an opportunity to ask a more useful question:

Which physical properties of sea ice are responsible for what we observe from space?

Answering that question is essential if satellite observations are going to provide more than maps. We want them to tell us something physically meaningful about the evolving ice cover and its interaction with the ocean.

From sea-ice observations to ocean dynamics

This also connects directly with my current research on the marginal ice zone.

Sea ice changes how ocean waves propagate. Waves, in turn, can fragment the ice and influence the energy available to drive upper-ocean mixing. The physical state of the ice therefore matters not only for remote-sensing retrievals, but also for the dynamics of the ocean beneath it.

A major goal of my current work is to identify which satellite-observable sea-ice and wave properties help explain this variability.

What I am looking at now

I am currently exploring early NISAR L-band observations alongside coincident Sentinel-1 C-band data over the Antarctic marginal ice zone.

The comparison provides a natural extension of my earlier multifrequency SAR work while moving into a region where the interaction between waves, sea-ice structure, and retrieval uncertainty raises a different set of questions.

What I find most compelling about new missions like NISAR is therefore not simply the arrival of another dataset. It is the possibility of observing the same physical system in several fundamentally different ways.

That combination is what can eventually help us connect satellite signals with the physics of a rapidly changing polar ocean.
